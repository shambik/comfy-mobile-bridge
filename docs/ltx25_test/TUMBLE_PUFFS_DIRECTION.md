## Series premise

Three tiny fluff creatures live in Bounce Patch, an oversized garden playground.
Every ordinary object is an invitation to play: a cushion, a garden hose, a bell,
a berry, a paper hat. Each character has a different idea about how to use it.
Their competing ideas produce one escalating visual joke, followed by a friendly
payoff and a final small surprise.

Bright polished 3D cartoon animation. Soft, visibly fluffy short fur, appealing
shapes, expressive eyes and brows, springy squash and stretch, strong poses,
sunny lighting and colourful uncluttered backgrounds. Camera framing leaves
room around the bodies for action and clear silhouettes. Expressive comedy and
playful energy drive the scene.

## Cast proposal

| Character | Visual identity | Behaviour that creates comedy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pip | Small coral pear shape, one curled tuft, tiny mitten paws and two broad feet | Acts immediately, celebrates too early, then notices the consequence |
| Momo | Wide lemon-yellow rounded cushion shape, tiny top tuft, heavier squash | Gentle and patient; solves a delicate problem with far too much weight |
| Tiki | Tall mint-green jellybean silhouette, two side tufts, springy stance | Loves arranging things precisely; every tidy solution becomes a new game |

Their personalities must be obvious from their poses and reactions. Clothing,
fine accessories and complicated hand anatomy are unnecessary to this design.

## Comedy structure

One location and one main prop per short. The audience sees the goal immediately.

1. Desire: one creature wants to do something simple.
2. Attempt: a clear physical action produces an unexpected result.
3. Escalation: a friend tries a contrasting solution and makes it bigger.
4. Payoff: their personalities collide in one strong visual gag.
5. Button: a small final reversal after they think it is over.

Sound consists of playful nonverbal reactions, clear comic foley and one coherent
musical score. Sound accents follow specific actions. Facial holds and reaction
shots give the audience time to understand each joke.

## First short: Too Bouncy

A red cushion sits in a sunny garden clearing.

- Pip presses it with one paw. It springs back and gently bops Pip's chin.
  Pip pauses, then grins: this is a toy.
- Pip makes a little hop onto it. The cushion squashes, then lifts Pip into
  a short delighted bounce. Tiki watches, unimpressed.
- Tiki carefully centres the cushion and demonstrates one elegant bounce.
  Pip copies the landing and nudges it off centre.
- Momo waddles over and sits on the cushion. It flattens completely. Pip and
  Tiki stare at the suddenly motionless cushion.
- Momo rises to investigate. The cushion slowly regains its shape and tips
  Momo backwards onto a soft grass mound. A surprised face becomes a giggle.
- The friends settle beside the cushion. A tiny butterfly lands on it and
  gets the smallest possible bounce. All three follow it upward with their eyes.

This is a proposed gag outline, not a claim that generated physics will be
correct. The first animated sample should establish the actual motion language
before expanding the short.

## New construction approach

- Start with one character lineup image that makes the proposed visual style
  concrete. The user can redirect the look immediately.
- Animate one short, readable six-second gag using at most two characters.
  This establishes expression, comic timing, fur and body motion in real output.
- Develop the first short to approximately 30–45 seconds, around six to eight
  shots. Plan each shot around one main action and its reaction.
- Use normal editorial cuts: an establishing view, action shots, close reactions
  and a final wide payoff. Opening frames establish identities and composition.
  Add a closing anchor only where the action specifically needs it.
- Write the target pose, action direction and reaction plainly. Avoid packing
  several rooms, a journey and multiple physical gags into one generation.
- Reuse the approved cast and location designs. Keep camera coverage purposeful
  so an edit can connect shots without demanding a continuous simulated take.
- Generate all still images directly with Codex's built-in ImageGen, as the
  user clarified. Submit LTX video renders through the H3 Mobile Bridge app.
  Keep existing runtime and saved workflows intact. Preserve prompts, seeds
  where available, and output files.
- Keep basic media validity checks. The user supplies visual feedback; no
  automatic creative rejection loop or extended comparison batch is required.
